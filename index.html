DSC 106 / Project 2

Progress or Inequality

Four countries. Two perspectives. The same World Bank indicators — and the design choices that change the story.

Group members: Muska Mesdaq (mmmesdaq@ucsd.edu) · Ryan Kasem (rkasem@ucsd.edu) · Ayesha Asim (aasim@ucsd.edu)
PropositionSupporting perspectiveOpposing perspectiveFinal reflection

Proposition

Between 2000 and 2020, the United States, Brazil, India, and Ethiopia made substantial progress toward universal access to basic drinking water and sanitation.

Scope: This proposition concerns four selected countries, not the world as a whole. The United States provides a near-universal-access benchmark; the other countries show larger changes. World Bank environment.csv indicators are AR (at least basic drinking water), AS (at least basic sanitation), and AQ (open defecation). The metadata file alone does not contain these measurements. “Basic” access does not necessarily mean safely managed or fully safe water.

Reading the argument: The supporting side highlights the magnitude of improvements; the opposing side asks whether the improvements are sufficient given unequal outcomes in 2020. Each perspective below is presented as a two-panel visualization, with each panel’s design decisions documented.

For the proposition

The gains are real.

Over two decades, access to basic water and sanitation expanded in several of the selected countries, and open defecation declined. The supporting charts make that change the focus.

Figure 01

Two Decades of Progress

Design decisions and rationale (5 decisions)
  1. Compared 2000 to 2020 instead of a single yearScore +1

    I used a dumbbell chart that connects each country’s 2000 value to its 2020 value, because my argument is about change over time and the gap between the two dots makes that change easy to see. Showing only the two endpoints hides the year-to-year path, but the overall trend in this data is steady, so not much is lost. I considered a line chart with all eight series (four countries for water and four for sanitation), but it was too cluttered to read.

  2. Labeled gains in percentage points and as a multiple for Ethiopia’s sanitationScore −1

    Each country’s endpoints are labeled by the size of the change, including India’s approximately 56-point sanitation gain. Ethiopia’s sanitation gain is also marked “(3×),” which makes an increase from about 3% to 9% sound more dramatic than the final level warrants. This framing is accurate in relative terms but potentially misleading if viewers overlook how low access remained.

  3. Greyed out the United States as “~100%”Score −0.5

    I used a muted color and the “~100%” label for the United States because it was already near universal access. That deemphasizes its slight sanitation decline from 99.9% to 99.7%. The U.S. drinking-water baseline is from 2005 because 2000 is unavailable, as disclosed in the figure note. Keeping the U.S. preserves a benchmark but can make the progress elsewhere look more dramatic.

  4. Used full percentage axes across both panelsScore +1.5

    Both supporting panels show the full 0–100% percentage range, with some extra room for change annotations. This avoids exaggerating gains through a truncated axis and makes the endpoints easier to interpret. I considered zooming in on Brazil’s smaller gains but decided that would be less transparent.

  5. Highlighted large gains with direct annotationsScore −0.5

    The chart labels India’s roughly 56-point sanitation improvement directly, rather than placing it in a separate oversized headline. This is persuasive because it highlights the largest improvement without pretending it is typical of all four countries. I considered an additional headline statistic but avoided repeating the same value in two places.

Figure 02

The Decline of Open Defecation

Design decisions and rationale (5 decisions)
  1. Paired open defecation with basic sanitation accessScore +1.5

    I paired open defecation (AQ) with basic sanitation access (AS) because falling open defecation alone does not fully establish progress in sanitation services. Showing both indicators for Brazil, India, and Ethiopia responds to the professor’s feedback and gives a broader view of change. The measures are related but not interchangeable.

  2. Showed both relative and percentage-point change without a misleading headlineScore −1

    I changed the original “fallen by 80%” headline to a more neutral title that names both sanitation indicators. India’s open defecation fell from about 74% to 15%—a decline of 59 percentage points, or about 80% relative to its starting value. The endpoints remain visible, so readers can distinguish those two interpretations.

  3. Omitted the near-zero U.S. series from the second comparisonScore −0.5

    I omitted the United States from this figure because its open-defecation rate was already near zero and its sanitation access was nearly universal. The omission lets the reader compare countries with more visible changes, but it also removes an important high-access benchmark. The first supporting chart and both opposing charts retain the United States.

  4. Compared three countries with different sanitation trajectoriesScore +1

    I show Brazil, India, and Ethiopia in both panels rather than focusing only on the two largest open-defecation declines. India and Ethiopia still draw attention because their reductions were substantial, while Brazil provides context for a country that started with higher sanitation access. I removed the world-average series to keep the comparison within the four-country scope of the proposition.

  5. Used full percentage scales and direct endpoint labelsScore +1.5

    Both panels use a 0–100% percentage range with direct 2000 and 2020 endpoint labels. This makes the size and direction of each change clear without inventing a smooth trend between years. The comparison is persuasive but leaves out year-to-year fluctuations.

Against the proposition

But the gaps are still wide.

A different reading of the same indicators centers what remained unresolved in 2020. These charts show how far the selected countries were from equal access, rather than how far they had come.

Figure 03

The Drinking Water Divide Persists

Design decisions and rationale (4 decisions)
  1. Selected four countries with different access levelsScore +0.5

    I chose the United States, Brazil, India, and Ethiopia because their drinking-water access rates show how different the situation can be depending on the country. This makes the gap more noticeable, although selecting only four countries does not represent the entire world. I considered using more countries, but that would make the chart harder to read.

  2. Focused only on 2020Score −1

    I used 2020 instead of showing changes over time because I wanted readers to focus on the problems that still exist. This makes the differences between countries more noticeable, but it also leaves out the improvements that happened in earlier years. I considered including multiple years, but I felt that focusing on one year would make my argument clearer.

  3. Used a horizontal bar chartScore +1.5

    I chose a horizontal bar chart because it makes the countries easy to compare. I kept a 0% to 100% scale for the transformed lack-of-access rates, so the bars show the true size of each shortfall. A line chart would have suggested a time trend, but this visualization intentionally compares a single year.

  4. Used a broader title and a lack-of-access transformationScore −0.5

    I used the title “The Drinking Water Divide Persists” to frame the differences across all four countries rather than only Ethiopia. I transformed basic drinking-water access (AR) into the percentage lacking access (100 − AR), so the chart emphasizes unmet need. This is a valid calculation but shifts attention away from the majority who do have access and from historical gains.

Figure 04

The Sanitation Divide Remains Wide

Design decisions and rationale (4 decisions)
  1. Compared the same four countriesScore +1

    I used the same countries as the drinking-water chart so readers could compare the two services. This also helps show that having access to drinking water does not automatically mean having access to sanitation. I considered selecting different countries, but keeping them consistent made the two visualizations easier to compare.

  2. Focused on remaining access gaps instead of improvementScore −1

    I only showed sanitation access in 2020 because my argument focuses on how many people still lack these services. Although sanitation access has improved over time, leaving out those earlier years makes the remaining problems stand out more. I considered showing changes over time, but that would shift the focus away from the inequalities that still exist.

  3. Used scales appropriate to each measureScore +2

    The sanitation-access panel uses a full 0–100% percentage axis, while the calculated water-minus-sanitation gap uses its own percentage-point scale beginning at zero. These are different quantities, so labeling them separately avoids implying they have the same units. I considered giving both panels the same upper bound, but that would make the smaller gap bars less legible.

  4. Emphasized cross-country differences and calculated a service gapScore −0.5

    I used the title “The Sanitation Divide Remains Wide” to emphasize disparities across all four countries. Alongside sanitation access (AS), I calculated the percentage-point difference between drinking-water and sanitation access (AR − AS). This adds a second perspective on uneven service coverage, but it should not be interpreted as the exact share of individuals who have water and lack sanitation.

Reflection / Ethics of persuasion

What the two stories taught us

For this project, we used the same World Bank environment dataset to argue both sides of one proposition: that between 2000 and 2020, the United States, Brazil, India, and Ethiopia made substantial progress toward universal access to basic drinking water and sanitation. We used the same four countries and the same indicators: drinking water access, sanitation access, and open defecation. The supporting side compared 2000 with 2020 to show how much access increased and open defecation declined. The opposing side focused on 2020, so the gaps between countries would stand out. Sorting out actual country rows from aggregates such as “World” and “Sub-Saharan Africa” was fairly straightforward, and we focused on the four selected countries rather than using a world average, since our proposition is specifically about those countries. Endpoint comparison charts worked well for showing change, while bar charts worked well for comparing countries in one year.

The hard part was choosing which countries, years, and wording to use, because those decisions mattered as much as the charts themselves. Emphasizing the remaining gaps across all four countries made the unequal outcomes clear, while showing only 2020 left out the historical progress that the supporting side needed. What surprised us most was how much titles and labels changed the message even when the numbers were accurate. Ethiopia’s sanitation access rose from about 3% to 9%. The supporting chart described that as “3×,” which sounds like major progress, while the opposing sanitation chart shows 8.9% access, which is fewer than 1 in 10 people. Both statements are true. Similarly, describing India’s decline in open defecation as about 80% sounds different from saying it fell by 59 percentage points. After our professor pointed out that open defecation alone was too narrow to support a broader sanitation argument, we added basic sanitation access to the second supporting visualization and compared the two indicators side by side. We also learned that coloring the United States grey or leaving it out of one chart can direct attention without changing any values.

Before this project, we thought a chart was ethical as long as its numbers were correct and the axes were not cut off. Now we think that is not enough. Ethical visualization also means being clear about the scope of a claim and about the context readers would need to judge it fairly. Our opposing charts leave out progress over time, while our supporting charts give less attention to how far some countries still are from universal access. We think it is acceptable to use color, titles, or annotations to highlight a real pattern, but it becomes misleading when relative percentages are confused with percentage points or four selected countries are treated as the whole world. We revised our proposition to name the four countries after the professor raised this concern. Even when missing data and the U.S. 2005 baseline are disclosed in source notes, readers may miss those details. Our biggest takeaway is that accurate data can still be presented in a biased way, so being transparent about what we include and leave out really matters.
